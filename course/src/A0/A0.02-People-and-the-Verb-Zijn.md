# A0.2 People: Pronouns and the Verb zijn (Wie ben jij?)

## Unit at a glance

In this lesson (about 6 hours) you learn the words for people: **ik, jij, u, hij, zij, wij, jullie** and **zij**. You also learn the most important verb in Dutch, **zijn** (to be). With these two pieces you can say who someone is, what they do, where they're from and how they feel, and you can ask simple questions. You'll also learn when to say **u** and when to say **jij**, and the famous Dutch **g**.

**You will be able to:**

- Use all the subject pronouns, including the short forms (je, ze, we)
- Use every form of **zijn**: ik ben, jij bent, hij is, wij zijn...
- Say your job, your nationality and how you feel (Ik ben student. Ik ben moe.)
- Ask yes/no questions with zijn (Ben jij Sara? Bent u de docent?)
- Choose between **u** (formal) and **jij/je** (informal), and greet people at any time of day
- Pronounce **g/ch**, **sch** and **j**

**Builds on:** A0.1 (letters, short and long vowels, the pattern WHO + VERB + REST, ik ben / ik heet / ik kom uit).

## Part 1: The big picture

In A0.1 you built **Ik ben Sara**. That sentence has a person (**ik**) and a verb (**ben**). Today you'll replace **ik** with any other person, and see how the verb changes to match.

English does this with "to be" too: I **am**, you **are**, she **is**. Dutch works the same way, and the forms are close: **ben, bent, is, zijn**.

| English | Dutch |
| --- | --- |
| I am | ik ben |
| you are | jij bent |
| he is | hij is |
| she is | zij is |
| we are | wij zijn |
| they are | zij zijn |

**Key idea:** the verb must match the person. You can't say "ik is" in Dutch, just as you can't say "I is" in English. Learn the pronoun and its verb form together, as one unit: **ik ben, jij bent, hij is**.

### Quick check

1. Dutch for "I am" is ___ [[ik ben]]
2. Dutch for "he is" is ___ [[hij is]]
3. Which matches **ik**: ben or is? [[ben]]

## Part 2: Step 1: Who? The pronouns

### What you need to know

A pronoun is a short word that stands for a person: I, you, he, she, we, they. These are the Dutch ones:

| Person | Dutch (full form) | Short form | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 singular | ik | (none) | I |
| 2 singular, informal | jij | je | you (a friend, a child, someone your age) |
| 2 singular, formal | u | (none) | you (a stranger, an older person, at work) |
| 3 singular | hij | (none) | he |
| 3 singular | zij | ze | she |
| 3 singular | het | (none) | it |
| 1 plural | wij | we | we |
| 2 plural | jullie | (none) | you (more than one person) |
| 3 plural | zij | ze | they |

### Three things to notice

**Rule:** most pronouns have a **full form** and a **short form**. Use the short form normally (**je, ze, we**). Use the full form when you stress the person, for contrast: "**I** am tired, **you** aren't" or in a short question like **En jij?** (And you?).

**Rule:** Dutch has two words for "you" when you talk to one person. **jij/je** is informal, for friends, family, children and people your age in relaxed places. **u** is formal and polite, for strangers, older people, officials and customers. You'll practise choosing in Step 5.

**Careful:** **zij** and **ze** can mean "she" or "they". The verb tells you which one: **ze is** = she is, **ze zijn** = they are. You'll see this clearly in Step 2.

**Note:** **jullie** is the "you" for a group of friends or family, like "you guys" or "y'all". For a group you'd call **u** one by one, you can also use **u**.

### Worked examples

| Situation | Pronoun |
| --- | --- |
| Talking about yourself | ik |
| Talking to your friend Tom | jij / je |
| Talking to a doctor you've just met | u |
| Talking about Pieter | hij |
| Talking about Sara | zij / ze |
| Talking about yourself and Sara | wij / we |
| Talking to Tom and Sara together | jullie |
| Talking about Tom and Sara | zij / ze |

## Part 3: Step 2: The verb zijn

### What you need to know

**zijn** means "to be". It's the most used verb in Dutch, and it's irregular, just like English "to be". It has only four forms to learn: **ben, bent, is, zijn**.

| Pronoun | zijn | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ik | ben | I am |
| jij / je | bent | you are |
| u | bent | you are (formal) |
| hij / zij / ze / het | is | he / she / it is |
| wij / we | zijn | we are |
| jullie | zijn | you are (plural) |
| zij / ze | zijn | they are |

**Pattern:** the three plural persons (wij, jullie, zij) always use the same form, and it's the verb's dictionary form: **zijn**. This is true for **every** Dutch verb, so you'll see it again in A0.4.

**Rule:** **jij** and **u** share the same form: **bent**. **hij**, **zij** (she) and **het** share **is**.

### Worked examples

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| Ik ben Sara. | I'm Sara. |
| Je bent welkom. | You're welcome. (literally, welcome here) |
| U bent meneer De Vries. | You're Mr de Vries. |
| Hij is Pieter. | He's Pieter. |
| Ze is Ana. | She's Ana. |
| We zijn hier. | We're here. |
| Jullie zijn welkom. | You're welcome (all of you). |
| Ze zijn thuis. | They're at home. |

**Notice:** "ze is" means "she is" and "ze zijn" means "they are". The verb tells you which **ze** is meant.

## Part 4: Step 3: What comes after zijn

### What you need to know

After **zijn** you can put four kinds of words. Each one gives you a different kind of sentence.

**Pattern:** PERSON + ZIJN + name / job / nationality / how or where you are.

| What comes after | Example | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a name | Ik ben Kofi. | I'm Kofi. |
| a job | Ik ben student. | I'm a student. |
| a nationality | Ik ben Nigeriaans. | I'm Nigerian. |
| a feeling or a place | Ik ben moe. Ik ben thuis. | I'm tired. I'm at home. |

**Rule:** a job has **no word for "a"** after zijn. English says "I'm **a** student", Dutch says **Ik ben student**. This is the most common beginner mistake, so watch for it.

**Rule:** nationalities are written with a capital letter, like in English: **Nederlands**, **Engels**.

## Part 5: Step 4: Asking questions with zijn

### What you need to know

To turn a statement into a yes/no question, **swap the person and the verb**. That's all: no extra word like English "do".

| Statement | Question |
| --- | --- |
| U bent de docent. | Bent u de docent? |
| Hij is moe. | Is hij moe? |
| Jullie zijn klaar. | Zijn jullie klaar? |
| Ze is thuis. | Is ze thuis? |

**Rule:** to ask a yes/no question, put the verb **first** and the person **second**.

**Careful:** with **jij** and **je**, the verb loses its **-t** when it comes first. **Jij bent** becomes **Ben jij?**, not "Bent jij?". This happens only with jij/je, never with u: **Bent u?** keeps its t. You'll see the same rule with every verb in A0.5.

| Statement | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Jij bent Tom. | Ben jij Tom? |
| Je bent moe. | Ben je moe? |
| U bent mevrouw De Vries. | Bent u mevrouw De Vries? |

### Answering

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| Ja. | Yes. |
| Nee. | No. |
| Ja, ik ben Tom. | Yes, I'm Tom. |
| Nee, ik ben Sam. | No, I'm Sam. |

**Note:** to say "I'm **not** tired" you need the word **niet**, which you learn in A0.3. For now, answer "no" by giving the right information: **Nee, ik ben Sam.**

### The question word wie (who)

**wie** means "who". Put it first, then the verb, then the person.

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| Wie ben jij? | Who are you? |
| Wie is dat? | Who's that? |
| Dat is Pieter. | That's Pieter. |
| Wie zijn jullie? | Who are you (all)? |

**Note:** **dat** means "that". **Dat is ...** (That's ...) is how you introduce someone: **Dat is Ana.**

### Practice: make it a question

Type the question. Remember to drop the **-t** after jij/je.

1. Hij is thuis. → ___ [[Is hij thuis?]]
2. U bent de docent. → ___ [[Bent u de docent?]]
3. Jij bent Tom. → ___ [[Ben jij Tom?]]
4. Jullie zijn klaar. → ___ [[Zijn jullie klaar?]]
5. Je bent moe. → ___ [[Ben je moe?]]
6. Ze is arts. → ___ [[Is ze arts?]]

### Practice: build the question

1. Are you Sara? {{Sara / jij / Ben}} [[Ben jij Sara?]]
2. Is he a student? {{hij / student / Is}} [[Is hij student?]]

4. Who are you? {{jij / Wie / ben}} [[Wie ben jij?]]
5. Who's that? {{dat / is / Wie}} [[Wie is dat?]]
6. That's Ana. {{Ana / is / Dat}} [[Dat is Ana.]]

## Part 6: Step 5: Greetings, u or jij?

### What you need to know

Greetings change with the time of day and with how formal the situation is. The formal ones go with **u**, the informal ones with **jij/je**.

| Dutch | English | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goedemorgen | Good morning | Until 12.00 |
| Goedemiddag | Good afternoon | 12.00 to about 18.00 |
| Goedenavond | Good evening | From about 18.00 |
| Hallo | Hello | Any time, neutral |
| Hoi | Hi | Any time, informal |
| Dag | Hello / bye | Any time, neutral |

| Dutch | English | Style |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tot ziens | Goodbye | Formal and neutral |
| Doei | Bye | Informal |
| Tot morgen | See you tomorrow | Neutral |
| Dank u wel | Thank you very much | Formal, goes with u |
| Dank je wel | Thank you very much | Informal, goes with je |

### Fixed phrases you can use now

These phrases use grammar you'll learn later, so learn them as whole chunks for now.

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| Hoe gaat het? | How are you? (literally: how goes it?) |
| Goed, dank je. | Fine, thanks. |
| En met jou? / En met u? | And you? (informal / formal) |
| Waar kom je vandaan? | Where are you from? (informal) |
| Waar komt u vandaan? | Where are you from? (formal) |
| Aangenaam. | Pleased to meet you. (formal) |
| meneer / mevrouw | Mr, sir / Mrs, Ms, madam |

**Rule:** say **u** to adults you don't know, at work when you've just met, to officials, and in shops if you're unsure. Say **jij/je** to friends, family, children and people your age in relaxed places. If someone says "Zeg maar je" (just say je), switch to je.

### Worked example: formal (at the office)

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mevrouw de Vries | Goedemorgen. Bent u David? | Good morning. Are you David? |
| David | Ja. Goedemorgen, mevrouw De Vries. Ik ben David Okafor. | Yes. Good morning, Mrs de Vries. I'm David Okafor. |
| Mevrouw de Vries | Aangenaam. Waar komt u vandaan? | Pleased to meet you. Where are you from? |
| David | Ik kom uit Nigeria. Ik ben ingenieur. | I'm from Nigeria. I'm an engineer. |
| Mevrouw de Vries | Welkom! | Welcome! |
| David | Dank u wel. | Thank you very much. |

### Worked example: informal (at a party)

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lotte | Hoi! Ik ben Lotte. Wie ben jij? | Hi! I'm Lotte. Who are you? |
| Sam | Hoi Lotte, ik heet Sam. | Hi Lotte, my name's Sam. |
| Lotte | Hoe gaat het? | How are you? |
| Sam | Goed, dank je. En met jou? | Fine, thanks. And you? |
| Lotte | Goed! Waar kom je vandaan? | Good! Where are you from? |
| Sam | Ik kom uit Engeland. Ik ben student. | I'm from England. I'm a student. |
| Lotte | Leuk! Doei! | Nice! Bye! |

## Part 7: Sounds: g, ch, sch and j

### The Dutch g and ch

In the Netherlands, **g** and **ch** are the same sound: a rough, breathy "kh" made at the back of the mouth, like the "ch" in Scottish "loch" or German "Bach". It's never the hard "g" of English "go".

**How to make it:** say "k", but don't close your throat completely. Let the air rub through the gap. It should sound like a soft hiss from the back of your mouth.

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| goed | good |
| dag | hello, day |
| Goedemorgen | good morning |
| acht | eight |
| de dochter | daughter |

**Note:** in Belgium and the south of the Netherlands the g is softer and "smoother". Both are correct. This course models the Netherlands sound and you'll hear the Flemish one from A2.

### sch

**sch** is **s** + the **ch** sound. It's never English "sk" or "sh". **School** sounds like "s-khool".

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| de school | school |
| de schoen | shoe |
| Scheveningen | a beach near The Hague |

**Careful:** at the end of a word, **-isch** is just "-ies": **praktisch** (practical) sounds like "prak-ties". The ch is silent there.

### j

The Dutch **j** sounds like English **y** in "yes": **ja** (yes) sounds like "yah", **jij** like "yay".

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| ja | yes |
| jij | you |
| jullie | you (plural) |
| Jan | Jan (a name) |
