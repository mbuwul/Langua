# A0.5 Word Order and Questions (Het werkwoord op plaats twee)

## Unit at a glance

This lesson (about 7 hours) teaches the rule that makes a sentence sound Dutch: **the verb is always in second place**. You'll learn to start sentences with a time or a place (**Vandaag werk ik thuis**), to ask yes/no questions with any verb (**Woon je in Utrecht?**), and to ask with question words (**Waar woon je? Wat drink je?**). You'll also count to 1,000 and handle prices, so you can shop.

**You will be able to:**

- Keep the verb in second place, even when the sentence starts with a time or a place
- Ask yes/no questions with any verb, including the jij rule (Werk jij? not "Werkt jij?")
- Ask questions with wie, wat, waar, waar ... vandaan, wanneer, hoe and hoeveel
- Say any number from 0 to 1,000, and ask and say prices
- Pronounce the Dutch **w**, **r** and the **-ig** ending

**Builds on:** A0.1 to A0.4 (pronouns, zijn, hebben, regular verbs, de/het/een, niet/geen, numbers 0 to 20).

## Part 1: The big picture

Think of a Dutch sentence as a row of boxes. The verb always sits in **box 2**.

| Box 1 | Box 2: VERB | Box 3 | The rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ik | werk | | vandaag thuis. |
| Vandaag | werk | ik | thuis. |
| Thuis | werk | ik | vandaag. |

All three sentences mean "I'm working at home today". Box 1 can hold the person, a time or a place. Whatever is in box 1, the verb stays in box 2, and if the person isn't in box 1, it moves to box 3, straight after the verb.

**Key idea:** English sometimes does this too: "Here **comes** the bus", "Never **have** I seen that". In Dutch it isn't occasional. It happens in **every** statement.

Questions use the same boxes: in a yes/no question, box 1 is empty, so the verb comes first. In a question with a question word, the question word goes in box 1.

| Box 1 | Box 2: VERB | Box 3 | The rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | Werk | je | vandaag? |
| Waar | werk | je | vandaag? |

## Part 2: Step 1: The verb is always second

### What you need to know

You already know sentences that start with the person: **Ik woon in Utrecht.** Now you can start with something else, to put it in focus or link it to what came before.

**Rule:** if anything other than the person starts the sentence, swap the person and the verb. Verb in box 2, person in box 3.

### Words that often start a sentence

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| vandaag | today |
| morgen | tomorrow |
| nu | now |
| dan | then |
| 's ochtends | in the morning |
| 's middags | in the afternoon |
| 's avonds | in the evening |
| hier | here |
| daar | there |
| in Nederland | in the Netherlands |
| ook | also, too |
| vaak | often |

### Worked examples

| Person first | Something else first | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ik werk vandaag. | Vandaag werk ik. | Today I'm working. |
| Hij kookt 's avonds. | 's Avonds kookt hij. | In the evening he cooks. |
| We wonen in Gent. | In Gent wonen we. | We live in Ghent. |
| Ze is morgen thuis. | Morgen is ze thuis. | Tomorrow she's at home. |
| Ik heb nu tijd. | Nu heb ik tijd. | Now I have time. |
| Ik drink 's ochtends koffie. | 's Ochtends drink ik koffie. | In the morning I drink coffee. |

**Watch out:** the English habit is to say "Vandaag ik werk" (today I work). That's the mistake every English speaker makes, often for years. Say the right version aloud many times now, until "Vandaag werk ik" sounds normal.

**Note:** **'s ochtends**, **'s middags**, **'s avonds** start with an apostrophe. At the start of a sentence, the capital goes on the next letter: **'s Avonds**.

## Part 3: Step 2: Yes/no questions with any verb

### What you need to know

In A0.2 you made questions with zijn: **Ben je moe?** Every verb works the same way: put the verb first.

**Rule:** for a yes/no question, the verb goes in box 2 with box 1 left empty, so the verb is the first word.

| Statement | Question | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hij werkt thuis. | Werkt hij thuis? | Does he work at home? |
| U woont in Gent. | Woont u in Gent? | Do you live in Ghent? |
| Ze spreekt Engels. | Spreekt ze Engels? | Does she speak English? |
| Jullie hebben een auto. | Hebben jullie een auto? | Do you have a car? |

**Careful:** the **jij rule**. When **jij** or **je** comes straight after the verb, the verb loses its **-t**. This happens in questions, and also in statements like **Vandaag werk je thuis**.

| Statement | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Jij werkt thuis. | Werk jij thuis? |
| Je woont in Gent. | Woon je in Gent? |
| Je spreekt Engels. | Spreek je Engels? |
| Jij hebt een fiets. | Heb jij een fiets? |
| Je bent moe. | Ben je moe? |

The rule only applies to **jij/je**. With **u**, **hij** and **zij** the -t stays: **Werkt u? Werkt hij?**

### Practice: make it a question

1. Hij woont in Utrecht. → ___ [[Woont hij in Utrecht?]]
2. Jij werkt vandaag. → ___ [[Werk jij vandaag?]]
3. U spreekt Nederlands. → ___ [[Spreekt u Nederlands?]]
4. Je drinkt koffie. → ___ [[Drink je koffie?]]
5. Ze leest een boek. → ___ [[Leest ze een boek?]]

7. Je hebt tijd. → ___ [[Heb je tijd?]]
8. Jij leert Nederlands. → ___ [[Leer jij Nederlands?]]

## Part 4: Step 3: Question words

### What you need to know

To ask for information, put a question word in box 1. The verb follows in box 2 and the person in box 3, exactly like a statement starting with "today".

**Pattern:** QUESTION WORD + VERB + PERSON + REST?

| Question word | English | Example | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| wie | who | Wie is dat? | Who's that? |
| wat | what | Wat drink je? | What are you drinking? |
| waar | where | Waar woon je? | Where do you live? |
| waar ... vandaan | where ... from | Waar kom je vandaan? | Where are you from? |
| wanneer | when | Wanneer werk je? | When do you work? |
| hoe | how | Hoe heet je? | What's your name? (how are you called?) |
| hoe oud | how old | Hoe oud ben je? | How old are you? |
| hoeveel | how much, how many | Hoeveel kinderen heb je? | How many children do you have? |

**Remember:** the jij rule works here too: **Waar woon je?** (no t), but **Waar woont u?** and **Waar woont hij?**

**Note:** now you can see how **Waar kom je vandaan?** from A0.2 is built: **waar** in box 1, **kom** in box 2, **je** in box 3, and **vandaan** (from) at the end.

**Careful:** "What's your name?" is **Hoe heet je?** (literally "How are you called?"), not "Wat heet je?".

### Worked example: getting to know someone

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lisa | Hoe heet je? | What's your name? |
| Omar | Ik heet Omar. En jij? | My name's Omar. And you? |
| Lisa | Lisa. Waar kom je vandaan? | Lisa. Where are you from? |
| Omar | Ik kom uit Marokko. Nu woon ik in Rotterdam. | I'm from Morocco. Now I live in Rotterdam. |
| Lisa | Wat doe je? | What do you do? |
| Omar | Ik ben kok. Ik werk in een restaurant. | I'm a chef. I work in a restaurant. |
| Lisa | Wanneer werk je? | When do you work? |
| Omar | 's Avonds. Overdag leer ik Nederlands. | In the evening. During the day I study Dutch. |

**New words:** **Wat doe je?** (What do you do? **doen** is one of the short verbs from A1.3), **het restaurant**, **overdag** (during the day).

## Part 5: Step 4: Numbers 21 to 1,000

### What you need to know

**Rule:** from 21 to 99, Dutch says the **unit first**, then **en** (and), then the ten: 21 = **een-en-twintig** ("one-and-twenty"). It's written as one word.

| Tens | Dutch | Tens | Dutch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | twintig | 60 | zestig |
| 30 | dertig | 70 | zeventig |
| 40 | veertig | 80 | tachtig |
| 50 | vijftig | 90 | negentig |

**Careful:** **veertig** (not viertig), **dertig** (not drietig) and **tachtig** (80, with a t at the start).

| Number | Dutch | Literally |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 21 | eenentwintig | one-and-twenty |
| 22 | tweeëntwintig | two-and-twenty |
| 35 | vijfendertig | five-and-thirty |
| 48 | achtenveertig | eight-and-forty |
| 63 | drieënzestig | three-and-sixty |
| 99 | negenennegentig | nine-and-ninety |

**Note:** the two dots in **tweeëntwintig** and **drieënzestig** (a trema) show where a new syllable starts: twee-en, drie-en. They're only needed after a word ending in e.

### Hundreds and a thousand

| Number | Dutch |
| --- | --- |
| 100 | honderd |
| 101 | honderdeen |
| 150 | honderdvijftig |
| 200 | tweehonderd |
| 365 | driehonderdvijfenzestig |
| 1000 | duizend |

**Rule:** no "and" after hundred, and no "one" before it: 100 is **honderd** (not "een honderd"), 1,000 is **duizend**.

## Part 6: Step 5: Prices and paying

### What you need to know

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| Wat kost dat? | What does that cost? |
| Hoeveel kost het? | How much is it? |
| Het kost drie euro. | It costs three euros. |
| De appels kosten twee euro. | The apples cost two euros. |
| Dat is dan ... | That'll be ... (at the till) |
| Alstublieft. | Here you are. / Please. (formal) |
| Alsjeblieft. | Here you are. / Please. (informal) |
| Dank u wel. | Thank you very much. |
| Pinnen, graag. | By card, please. |
| contant | cash |

**Rule:** a price is said as **euros + cents**, without "and" and without "cent": **€ 2,50** is **twee euro vijftig**. Under one euro: **€ 0,80** is **tachtig cent**.

**Rule:** **euro** doesn't get a plural ending: **drie euro**, never "drie euros".

**Careful:** Dutch writes a **comma** for decimals: **€ 2,50** (two euros fifty). A point separates thousands: **€ 1.000** (one thousand).

**Note:** **kosten** (to cost) is a regular verb: **het kost** (one thing), **ze kosten** (more than one).

### Worked examples

| Written | Said |
| --- | --- |
| € 1,20 | een euro twintig |
| € 3,75 | drie euro vijfenzeventig |
| € 12,99 | twaalf euro negenennegentig |
| € 0,50 | vijftig cent |
| € 45,– | vijfenveertig euro |

## Part 7: Sounds: w, r and -ig

### The Dutch w

The Dutch **w** isn't English "w". Touch your top teeth very lightly to your bottom lip, as for "v", but softer, with almost no friction. In the Netherlands it's between English "v" and "w".

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| wat | what |
| waar | where |
| wonen | to live |
| wie | who |
| de wijn | wine |

### The Dutch r

The **r** varies a lot. Two versions are both correct:

- a **tapped or rolled r** at the tip of the tongue, as in Spanish or Scottish English
- a **throaty r** at the back of the mouth, as in French or German

At the end of a word, many people in the Netherlands say a soft r close to American English: **waar**, **hier**. Use whichever r you can make. Just don't drop it: **waar** has a clear r.

### -ig at the end

**-ig** sounds like "-ukh": a schwa + the Dutch g. **twintig** = "TWIN-tukh", **dertig**, **veertig**, **tachtig**.

## Part 9: Listen & read

### Dialogue: Op de markt (At the market)

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verkoper | Goedemorgen! | Good morning! |
| Sara | Goedemorgen. Hoeveel kost de kaas? | Good morning. How much is the cheese? |
| Verkoper | De kaas kost zes euro vijftig. | The cheese is six euros fifty. |
| Sara | En het brood? | And the bread? |
| Verkoper | Het brood kost twee euro twintig. | The bread is two euros twenty. |
| Sara | Dat is goedkoop! Ik neem het brood. | That's cheap! I'll take the bread. |
| Verkoper | Heeft u een tas? | Do you have a bag? |
| Sara | Nee, ik heb geen tas. | No, I don't have a bag. |
| Verkoper | Geen probleem. Dat is dan twee euro twintig. Pinnen? | No problem. That'll be two euros twenty. By card? |
| Sara | Nee, contant. Alstublieft. | No, cash. Here you are. |
| Verkoper | Dank u wel. Fijne dag! | Thank you very much. Have a nice day! |

**New words:** **de verkoper** (seller), **geen probleem** (no problem), **Fijne dag!** (Have a nice day!).
