# A0.1 How Dutch Works (Hoe werkt het Nederlands?)

## Unit at a glance

This first lesson (about 6 hours) gives you a map of the Dutch language before you learn a single list of words. You'll see why Dutch is easier for English speakers than it looks, learn the letters and the one spelling rule that unlocks Dutch pronunciation, and build your first real sentences: **Ik ben Sara. Ik kom uit Engeland.** Every step is explained first, then practised until it's automatic.

**You will be able to:**

- Explain the five big features of Dutch that the rest of the course builds on
- Say the Dutch alphabet and spell your name aloud

- Say three sounds English doesn't have: **uu**, **u** and the "lazy e" (schwa)
- Build three sentences about yourself: your name, what you're called, where you're from

## Part 1: The big picture

Before any words, here is how Dutch works. Keep this map in mind: every lesson in A0 fills in one piece of it.

### Dutch is a close cousin of English

Dutch and English come from the same family of languages. Thousands of words look and sound alike, so you already know more Dutch than you think. Listen to each word and guess what it means.

| Dutch | English |
| --- | --- |
| het water | water |
| de hand | hand |
| de arm | arm |
| het bed | bed |
| de bus | bus |
| de appel | apple |
| de koffie | coffee |
| de thee | tea |
| het huis | house |
| de man | man |
| de naam | name |
| warm | warm |

**Note:** the little words **de** and **het** in front of the nouns both mean "the". Dutch has two words for "the". Don't worry about them yet: you'll learn exactly how they work in A0.3.

### The five big features of Dutch

**Key idea 1: Spelling is regular.** Unlike English (think of "though", "through", "tough"), Dutch spelling follows rules. Once you know them, you can read any Dutch word aloud correctly, even one you've never seen. You start learning these rules in this lesson.

**Key idea 2: The verb changes for each person.** English says "I am, you are, he is". Dutch does the same with almost every verb: **ik woon** (I live), **jij woont** (you live). The good news: there are only three endings to learn. That's A0.2 and A0.4.

**Key idea 3: Every noun is a "de-word" or a "het-word".** You learn each noun together with its word for "the". That's A0.3.

**Key idea 4: The verb is always in second place.** In a Dutch statement, the verb is the second item, no matter what comes first. "Today I work" becomes **Vandaag werk ik** (literally "today work I"). That's A0.5, and it's the single most important rule of Dutch word order.

**Key idea 5: Questions start with the verb.** "You live here" is **Jij woont hier**. "Do you live here?" is **Woon jij hier?** No "do" is needed. That's A0.5 too.

## Part 2: Step 1: The letters

### What you need to know

Dutch uses the same 26 letters as English, but most letters have different names. You need the names to spell your name, your street and your email address, which you'll do many times in your first weeks in the Netherlands or Belgium.

| Letter | Dutch name sounds like | Letter | Dutch name sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | aa (as in "father") | n | en |
| b | bee (like "bay") | o | oo (like "oh") |
| c | see (like "say") | p | pee (like "pay") |
| d | dee (like "day") | q | kuu |
| e | ee (like "ay") | r | er |
| f | ef | s | es |
| g | gee (with the rough Dutch g) | t | tee (like "tay") |
| h | haa | u | uu (no English sound, see Step 3) |
| i | ie (like "ee") | v | vee (like "vay") |
| j | jee (like "yay") | w | wee (like "way") |
| k | kaa | x | iks |
| l | el | y | ij, or Griekse ij |
| m | em | z | zet |

**Watch out:** three letter names catch every English speaker. Dutch **a** sounds like English "ah". Dutch **e** sounds like English "ay". Dutch **i** sounds like English "ee". Say them slowly until it feels normal.

**Rule:** when you spell, say each letter's Dutch name. A double letter is said twice: **Anna** is "aa - en - en - aa". For a capital letter you can say **hoofdletter** (capital), but most people just spell the letters.

| Jan | jee - aa - en |
| Kees | kaa - ee - ee - es |

## Part 3: Step 2: Short and long vowels

### What you need to know

This is the most useful rule in this lesson. Dutch has short vowels and long vowels, and the difference changes the meaning of a word. **man** means "man", but **maan** means "moon". The spelling always tells you which one you're looking at.

**Rule 1:** a single vowel before a consonant at the end of a word is **short**. A double vowel is **long**.

| Written as | How it sounds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a + consonant | short a (like "o" in American "hot") | man, tak, kat |
| aa | long a (like "a" in "father", longer) | maan, taak, naam |
| e + consonant | short e (like "e" in "bed") | pen, bed, wet |
| ee | long e (like "ay" in "day", without the glide) | peen, beet, weet |
| o + consonant | short o (like "o" in British "hot") | bot, pot, rok |
| oo | long o (like "o" in "go", but steady) | boot, poot, rook |
| i + consonant | short i (like "i" in "bit") | vis, wit, lip |
| ie | long i (like "ee" in "see") | vies, wiel, liep |

**Notice:** the long i is never written **ii**. It's written **ie**. That's the only exception in the table.

**Why this matters:** you'll use this rule every day. It tells you how to read new words, and in A0.4 it explains how Dutch verbs are spelled.

### Worked examples: hear the difference

Each pair is the same word except for the length of the vowel. Listen to both, then say both.

| Short | English | Long | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| de man | man | de maan | moon |
| de tak | branch | de taak | task |
| de pen | pen | de peen | carrot |
| de wet | law | ik weet | I know |
| het bot | bone | de boot | boat |
| de rok | skirt | de rook | smoke |
| de vis | fish | vies | dirty |
| de lip | lip | ik liep | I walked |

### How to make a long vowel

1. **Hold it.** A long vowel lasts about twice as long as a short one: "maaan", not "man".
2. **Keep your mouth still.** English speakers glide at the end ("day-ee", "go-oo"). For Dutch **ee** and **oo**, freeze your lips and tongue from start to finish.
3. **Open your mouth for aa.** The Dutch long a is bright and open, like the doctor saying "say aah".

## Part 4: Step 3: Sounds English doesn't have

### What you need to know

Most Dutch vowels have a near match in English. Three don't, and these are the ones that give English speakers an accent. Learn them now and they won't trouble you later.

### The long uu: "ee" with round lips

Say English "ee". Keep your tongue exactly there and push your lips forward into a small circle, as if you were going to whistle. That's **uu**. It's the same sound as French "tu" or German "ü".

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| de muur | wall |
| duur | expensive |
| de buur | neighbour |
| het uur | hour |

### The short u: "i" in "bit" with round lips

Do the same with the short "i" of "bit": round your lips and keep the sound short. It sounds a little like the "u" in English "hurt", but shorter and without the r.

| Example | English |
| --- | --- |
| de bus | bus |
| dun | thin |
| de kus | kiss |
| de hut | hut, cabin |

### The lazy e (schwa)

In a syllable that isn't stressed, **e** is said as a short, relaxed "uh", like the "a" in English "about". It's the most common vowel in Dutch. You hear it in the little word **de** and in most word endings.

| Example | Sounds like | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| de | "duh" | the |
| lopen | "LOH-puh(n)" | to walk |
| de appel | "AP-puhl" | the apple |
| een | "un" | a, an |

**Tip:** in everyday speech in the Netherlands, the **-n** at the end of **-en** is often dropped: **lopen** sounds like "lopuh". Both are correct. This course models the clear version first.

### Two letters, one sound: ie and oe

Two common spellings look like two vowels but are just one sound:

| Written as | How it sounds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ie | "ee" in "see" | vies, wiel, Pieter |
| oe | "oo" in "food" | goed (good), hoe (how), het boek (book) |

**Careful:** **oe** is never "oh-ee". **Goed** sounds close to English "khoot", with the rough Dutch g you'll learn in A0.2.

## Part 5: Step 4: Your first sentence

### What you need to know

A sentence needs two things: **who** (the person doing something) and **a verb** (what they do or are). In Dutch, as in English, the "who" usually comes first and the verb comes second. Everything else comes after.

**Pattern:** WHO + VERB + REST. **Ik** (I) + **ben** (am) + **Sara** gives a complete Dutch sentence: **Ik ben Sara.**

Look at the three words one by one:

| Dutch | English | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ik | I | the person (pronoun) |
| ben | am | the verb (a form of zijn, "to be") |
| Sara | Sara | the rest of the sentence |

**Careful:** **ik** is written with a small letter, unlike English "I". It only gets a capital at the start of a sentence: **Ik ben Sara.** But: **Hallo, ik ben Sara.**

### Three sentences about yourself

You now get three verbs. Each one follows the same pattern, so you can build all three sentences straight away.

| Dutch | English | Literally |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ik ben Sara. | I'm Sara. | I am Sara. |
| Ik heet Sara. | My name is Sara. | I am called Sara. |
| Ik kom uit Engeland. | I'm from England. | I come out of England. |

**Note:** **ik heet** is the most common way to give your name. **heet** has a long **ee** (rule 1). **uit** (out, from) has the sound **ui**, which you'll learn properly in A0.3. For now, copy the speaker.
