# A1.3 Daily Routine (De dagelijkse routine)

## Unit at a glance

After this unit (about 9 hours) you can describe a normal day: when you get up, work, eat, travel and go to bed, and how often you do things. You'll learn how to use **any regular verb** in the present tense, and the most important word-order rule in Dutch: **the verb always comes second**. In pronunciation you learn the Dutch r, which comes in three versions.

**You will be able to:**

- Use about 30 everyday verbs in the present tense, with every pronoun
- Apply the spelling rules when a verb loses its -en (slapen → ik slaap)
- Start a sentence with a time word and put the verb second (Om acht uur werk ik.)
- Say how often you do things: altijd, meestal, vaak, soms, nooit
- Tell the time of day: 's ochtends, 's middags, 's avonds, 's nachts
- Say the Dutch r clearly and never drop it after a vowel

**Builds on:** A0.4 (regular verbs, spelling rules), A0.5 (verb second), A0.6 (time and days), A1.1 and A1.2.

## Part 1: Situation

### Dialogue 1: Een gewone dag (A normal day)

Sanne asks Kofi about his day. They use **je**.

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sanne | Hoe laat begin je met werken? | What time do you start work? |
| Kofi | Om half negen. Ik word om zes uur wakker. | At half past eight. I wake up at six. |
| Sanne | Zo vroeg! Wat doe je dan? | So early! What do you do then? |
| Kofi | Eerst douche ik. Dan ontbijt ik en lees ik het nieuws. | First I shower. Then I have breakfast and read the news. |
| Sanne | Hoe ga je naar je werk? | How do you get to work? |
| Kofi | Ik fiets. Het is twintig minuten. | I cycle. It's twenty minutes. |
| Sanne | En 's middags? | And at lunchtime? |
| Kofi | Ik luncht met mijn collega's. Nee, sorry: ik lunch met mijn collega's. | I have lunch with my colleagues. |
| Sanne | Goed zo! En 's avonds? | Well done! And in the evening? |
| Kofi | Om zes uur kook ik. Daarna bel ik mijn moeder in Ghana. | At six I cook. After that I call my mother in Ghana. |
| Sanne | Elke dag? | Every day? |
| Kofi | Nee, niet elke dag. Maar ik bel haar vaak. | No, not every day. But I call her often. |

**Notice:** Kofi corrects himself. With **ik**, the verb never gets a -t: ik lunch, ik werk, ik fiets.

### Dialogue 2: Bij het schoolhek (At the school gate)

Grace and Fatima, two mothers, wait for their children.

| Who | Dutch | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fatima | Hoi Grace! Je ziet er moe uit. | Hi Grace! You look tired. |
| Grace | Ja, ik slaap slecht. Leo wordt 's nachts vaak wakker. | Yes, I'm sleeping badly. Leo often wakes up at night. |
| Fatima | O, dat ken ik. Werk je vandaag? | Oh, I know that feeling. Are you working today? |
| Grace | Ja, ik werk van tien tot vier in het ziekenhuis. Mijn man haalt Leo op. | Yes, I work from ten till four at the hospital. My husband picks Leo up. |
| Fatima | Wij doen het zo: Ruben brengt de kinderen en ik haal ze. | We do it like this: Ruben takes the kids and I pick them up. |
| Grace | Slim! Hoe laat eten jullie? | Clever! What time do you eat? |
| Fatima | Altijd om zes uur. De kinderen gaan om half acht naar bed. | Always at six. The children go to bed at half past seven. |
| Grace | Wij eten meestal later. Tom komt pas om zeven uur thuis. | We usually eat later. Tom only gets home at seven. |

**Note:** "Je ziet er moe uit", "haalt op" and "komt thuis" are verbs in two pieces. Learn them as phrases for now. Unit A1.7 explains how they work.

**Check questions:**

1. What time does Kofi wake up? (a) 6:00 (b) 6:30 (c) 8:30. **Answer: a**
2. How does Kofi get to work? (a) by bus (b) by bike (c) on foot. **Answer: b**
3. What does Kofi do after cooking? (a) reads the news (b) calls his mother (c) goes to bed. **Answer: b**
4. Why is Grace tired? (a) she works at night (b) Leo wakes up at night (c) she cycles a lot. **Answer: b**
5. What time do Fatima's children go to bed? (a) 6:00 (b) 7:00 (c) 7:30. **Answer: c**

## Part 3: Grammar

**Remember:** regular verbs were taught in A0.4 and the verb-second rule in A0.5, so 3A and 3D are a refresher. **New in this unit:** more stem spelling cases (stems in -d), the short verbs **gaan, doen, staan**, and where words like **altijd, vaak, soms** go.

### 3A. The present tense of regular verbs

**Step 1: Find the stem.** Take the infinitive and remove **-en**: werken → **werk**.

**Step 2: Add the ending.**

| Person | Ending | werken | wonen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ik | stem | ik werk | ik woon |
| jij / je, u | stem + t | jij werkt, u werkt | jij woont, u woont |
| hij / zij / het | stem + t | hij werkt | zij woont |
| wij / we | infinitive | wij werken | wij wonen |
| jullie | infinitive | jullie werken | jullie wonen |
| zij / ze (they) | infinitive | zij werken | zij wonen |

So there are only three forms to know: **werk, werkt, werken**.

**One trap:** in the plural, jullie and zij look exactly like the infinitive (werken). In English "they work" has no ending, but in Dutch the plural **always** has -en.

### 3B. Spelling rules for the stem

When you remove -en, the stem often ends in a closed syllable. Use the rules from A0.4 so the vowel keeps its sound.

| Rule | Infinitive | Stem (ik) | hij form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long vowel: write it double | slapen, lopen, koken, eten | slaap, loop, kook, eet | slaapt, loopt, kookt, eet |
| Double consonant: write it once | bellen, zitten, beginnen | bel, zit, begin | belt, zit, begint |
| v at the end becomes f | schrijven, leven | schrijf, leef | schrijft, leeft |
| z at the end becomes s | lezen, reizen | lees, reis | leest, reist |

**Stems that already end in -t:** don't add a second t. Hij **zit**, zij **eet**, hij **ontbijt**.

**Stems that end in -d:** add -t anyway, even though you can't hear it. Ik **word**, hij **wordt**. Ik **rijd**, zij **rijdt**. Both sound like "t".

### 3C. Four irregular short verbs

| | gaan | doen | staan | komen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ik | ga | doe | sta | kom |
| jij, u, hij, zij | gaat | doet | staat | komt |
| wij, jullie, zij | gaan | doen | staan | komen |

These are short verbs whose stems end in a vowel (ga, doe, sta), and **komen** has a short o in the stem. Learn them as they are.

### 3D. The verb is always second

In a Dutch statement, the conjugated verb is **always the second element**. The first element can be the subject, a time phrase, or another word. If something else comes first, the subject moves to the position **right after the verb**.

| 1 | 2 (verb) | 3 (subject) | Rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ik | werk | | om negen uur. |
| Om negen uur | werk | ik. | |
| 's Avonds | kook | ik | voor mijn gezin. |
| Daarna | bel | ik | mijn moeder. |
| Mijn man | brengt | | de kinderen naar school. |
| Meestal | eten | wij | om zes uur. |

**Element 1 can be more than one word.** "Om negen uur" counts as one element, so the verb comes straight after it.

**English speakers' most common mistake:** "Om negen uur ik werk" copies English word order. In Dutch the verb must come before the subject.

**The jij rule again:** when **je/jij** comes straight after the verb, the -t disappears. 's Ochtends **werk je**. Daarna **bel je** je moeder. (You'll see this rule again in questions in A1.4.)

**Where do altijd, vaak and soms go?** After the verb (and after the subject, if it's behind the verb):

- Ik fiets **altijd** naar mijn werk.
- 's Avonds kijk ik **soms** tv.
- Hij belt **nooit**. (never)

### Typical mistakes for English speakers

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Om acht uur ik werk. | Om acht uur werk ik. | Verb second, subject after it |
| Ik werkt. | Ik werk. | No -t with ik |
| Zij werk (she) | Zij werkt. | hij/zij/het add -t |
| Jullie werkt. | Jullie werken. | Plural uses the infinitive |
| Ik slap. | Ik slaap. | Long vowel: write it double |
| Ik schrijv. | Ik schrijf. | v becomes f at the end |
| Hij eett. | Hij eet. | Stem already ends in -t |
| Hij word wakker. | Hij wordt wakker. | Stem in -d still gets -t |

## Part 4: Pronunciation

### 4A. Three kinds of r

Dutch speakers use different r sounds, and all of them are standard Dutch:

| Type | How to make it | Where you hear it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front r | Tap or roll the tip of your tongue, like Spanish or Scottish r | Flanders, much of the Netherlands, many older speakers |
| Back r | Vibrate the back of the tongue, like French r, but softer | Randstad cities, many younger speakers |
| Soft r after a vowel | A soft, curled r, close to American English | End of syllables in the Netherlands: maar, door, werk |

**What to do:** choose a front or back r at the start of syllables (**r**ood, b**r**oer, **r**ustig) and stay with it. After a vowel, you may use the soft r (maa**r**, wer**k**, doo**r**), but you **must** say it.

### 4B. Don't drop the r

British English drops the r after a vowel: "car" sounds like "cah". In Dutch the r is always there, and dropping it can change the word.

| Word 1 | Word 2 | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| maar (but) | ma (mum) | The r makes it a different word |
| door (through) | doe (do) | Different vowel and the r |
| werk (work) | wek (wake someone) | The r changes the meaning |
| kaart (card, map) | kaat (not a word) | Say the r before t |
| vader | vade (not a word) | -er ends with r |

## Part 8: Culture & review

### A Dutch day

- **Dinner at six:** many Dutch families eat their warm meal (het avondeten) at about 6 pm. Phoning someone between 6 and 7 can feel rude.
- **Bread for lunch:** lunch is usually a **boterham** (a slice of bread) with cheese, ham or **pindakaas** (peanut butter), often brought from home in a lunchbox, with a glass of milk or buttermilk.
- **Bikes everywhere:** about a quarter of all trips in the Netherlands are made by bike, and many people cycle to work in all weather.
- **Part-time work:** many Dutch people work four days a week or fewer. A father who has his day off with the children calls it his **papadag**.
- **The agenda:** Dutch people plan a lot, even social life. "Ik kijk even in mijn agenda" (I'll check my diary) is normal, even for a coffee with friends.
- **Flemish note:** in Belgium the evening meal is often still called **het avondmaal**, and you'll hear **boterhammen** at lunch too. An informal Flemish word you'll hear a lot is **goesting**: "Ik heb geen goesting" means "I don't feel like it".
